# The nine archetypes

This document explains the personality-classification system behind the silver-card reveal at the end of onboarding. It's meant to make a reader who asks "why these categories?" come away thinking *someone thought about this*, not *someone rolled a d12*.

## Why three axes: Hero, Shadow, Muse

The three top-level categories are **Jung's own triad of individuation**, not a marketing device. In *The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious* (1959), Jung names three archetypes that mediate between the conscious ego and the deeper Self:

- **Hero** — the ego on its journey toward mastery. What Jung also called the Persona: the face we turn toward the world and the will that moves us through it.
- **Shadow** — the rejected, unconscious material. Not morally "evil" — Jung is clear that the Shadow also contains normal instincts, creative impulses, and realistic insights that the ego has disowned.
- **Muse** — the Anima (or Animus): the contrasexual inner figure that bridges conscious and unconscious. Jung described it as the inner archetype classically personified as a *muse* — the figure that brings inspiration from the deeper psyche.

Every person carries all three. Individuation — Jung's word for the psychological work of a full life — is the process of *integrating* them. So when a user takes this quiz and "ends up" a Hero, the picture of that user isn't one-dimensional: they're just a Hero whose particular Shadow and Muse are along for the ride.

As a second register, Freud's structural model rhymes loosely: Id ≈ Shadow (unconscious drive), Ego ≈ Hero (conscious self in the world), Superego-directed ideals ≈ Muse (the inner figure that holds what we aspire toward). We don't lean on Freud heavily — his structural model is about internal conflict, Jung's triad is about integration — but the cross-register is intentional for readers with one lens or the other.

## Why three subtypes per axis: Pearson & Mark

The nine subtypes are drawn from **Carol Pearson and Margaret Mark's 12 archetypes** (*The Hero and the Outlaw*, 2001; built on Pearson's earlier *Awakening the Heroes Within*). Pearson's framework is the most rigorously-operationalized descendant of Jungian archetype theory — a full instrument (the PMAI, Pearson-Marr Archetype Indicator) was built from it, and it's the standard reference when people in psychology or branding talk about "archetypes" in a non-hand-wavy way.

Pearson clusters her twelve archetypes developmentally into three groups — **Ego**, **Soul**, and **Self** — which themselves trace Jung's individuation path. We take 9 of the 12, keeping the three that read most legibly as a tarot-card figure per axis and dropping Caregiver, Sage, and Destroyer (whose scene-language overlaps too much with what we kept):

| Our axis | Our subtype | Pearson's archetype | What they want |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hero | Warrior | Warrior | to prove, to overcome |
| Hero | Ruler | Ruler | to hold, to create order |
| Hero | Seeker | Seeker / Explorer | to find, to journey |
| Shadow | Trickster | Jester / Fool | to subvert, to play |
| Shadow | Outcast | Orphan | to be seen, to belong |
| Shadow | Witch | Magician (dark aspect) | to know, to transmute |
| Muse | Dreamer | Innocent | to trust, to imagine |
| Muse | Lover | Lover | to merge, to feel |
| Muse | Maker | Creator | to build, to transform |

The three Muse subtypes also rhyme with **Jung's four stages of Anima development** (Eve → Helen → Mary → Sophia). We collapse to three by folding Mary (the religious-ethical stage) into the Sophia-endpoint (wisdom-through-making), which is what Jung himself does in his later writing on the Creator-figure.

Within each axis, no two subtypes mean the same thing — a user shouldn't be able to read the titles and feel like "Warrior" and "Seeker" are just the same Hero with different paint. Warrior wants to prove themselves against a specific test; Seeker wants to find out what's out there. They're opposed, not adjacent.

## One of nine is the title. All three axes make the card.

Every user is scored across all nine archetypes. The archetype with the highest normalized score becomes their **title** — one of nine, shown on the card. But we *also* pick, independently, the user's strongest subtype within each of the three axes, even when that axis isn't dominant.

Those three subtypes compose the image itself.

This matters because Jung's central claim is that the psyche is **plural**. A person who reads as predominantly Muse still has a Hero-aspect and a Shadow-aspect, and leaving those out of the card would give them back a flattened, half-true picture. The composition grammar — Hero decides when, Muse decides who, Shadow decides where — means every card carries all three.

## Why time, character, and setting

The three scene axes aren't arbitrary. Each one is the symbolic register Jung (or his closest students) associates with that archetype:

- **Hero → time of day.** Joseph Campbell's monomyth (*The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, 1949) structures the hero's journey as a temporal sequence: the call comes at one hour, the ordeal at another, the return at another. Time is how a Hero *moves*. We anchor the three Hero subtypes at three hours — noon (the Warrior, fully exposed, nothing hidden), sunset (the Ruler, the golden hour of reign), midnight (the Seeker, when the stars are out and the unknown is at its thickest).

- **Muse → character.** The Anima in Jung is *always personified as a figure* — he describes four successive stages (Eve, Helen, Mary, Sophia) each embodied as a specific kind of person. So the Muse axis in our scene determines *what kind of figure* appears on the card: a young figure (Dreamer / Eve-stage), an aged figure (Lover / Helen-stage mature), or a figure with a familiar beast (Maker / Sophia-stage, in which the figure has drawn a non-human companion from the unconscious into daily life).

- **Shadow → setting.** In Jungian dream analysis the Shadow appears as a *place* — the dark forest, the underworld, the abandoned room, the empty city street. The Shadow is where you are when the ego is not in charge. So the Shadow axis determines the backdrop: cobbled moonlit street (Trickster's public disguise), stormy coast (Outcast's liminal edge), field of scattered stars (Witch's cosmic interior).

## The nine characters

**The Duelist (Hero-Warrior).** The Duelist is not the soldier; the soldier belongs to a cause. The Duelist is the figure who has chosen a specific test — a specific person, a specific cliff, a specific dawn — and will meet it alone. The armor is silvered, not black; this is a Hero, not a villain. "Duelist" is chosen over "Warrior" because Warrior implies an army; the Hero-Warrior archetype in Pearson is about *individually* proving worth, not collective service.

**The Regent (Hero-Ruler).** The Regent is Pearson's Ruler: the one who holds the room together, who bears the weight of responsibility so others don't have to. "Regent" rather than "King" or "Queen" because a regent rules *on behalf of* — the archetype is service-through-authority, not divine right. The antler crown is a deliberate pull away from the gold-coronet cliché; antlers are the crown the land gives you.

**The Cartographer (Hero-Seeker).** The Cartographer is the Seeker's most specific expression: someone who goes out into the unknown and comes back with it mapped. "Wayfarer," "Pioneer," "Adventurer" — all of these are too generic, they dissolve into each other. A cartographer has intellect, patience, and a specific product. The compass-and-telescope scene is the seeker's journey at its most composed.

**The Jester (Shadow-Trickster).** Jung's Trickster and Pearson's Jester/Fool: the figure who disrupts the ego's order by making it laugh. The Shadow-Trickster is not cruel — the chaos is play, and the play is a kind of truth-telling the Hero cannot do. Coins, masks, ravens. The ravens are trickster birds in basically every mythological system; their presence is a tell.

**The Stray (Shadow-Outcast).** Pearson's Orphan — the one who has been separated from the group, either by circumstance or by their own refusal to be held. "Stray" is chosen over "Orphan" because the latter is a role that happens *to* you; Stray has an edge of agency, of having walked out. The wolf at their side is not metaphor for loneliness; it's the companion you find when you're past the village.

**The Seeress (Shadow-Witch).** The Seeress is the dark side of Pearson's Magician: someone who knows things the ego cannot. "Witch" as a subtype label captures the archetype honestly; "Seeress" as a title captures its dignity. The bones and scrying pool are tarot-standard. The veil is doing work — it marks the figure as intermediary, someone who moves between visible and invisible orders.

**The Somnambulist (Muse-Dreamer).** The Somnambulist — the sleepwalker — is Pearson's Innocent viewed through Jung's first Anima stage (Eve): the psyche in its unguarded, trusting, dreaming state. The figure walks without knowing where, through a landscape that exists only from the inside. "Dreamer" is too domesticated for the tarot; "Somnambulist" is the Dreamer with one foot in a world no one else can see.

**The Troubadour (Muse-Lover).** Pearson's Lover; Jung's Anima at the Helen stage, where love is no longer only biological but is yearning, song, distance. "Lover" as a title is too generic — what does the figure actually *do*? A troubadour sings of love, often for someone they cannot have. That's the archetype precisely: the Muse-Lover is the one for whom the world is made of the beloved.

**The Alchemist (Muse-Maker).** Alchemy in Jung's later writing *is* the image of individuation — the work of transmuting base material into gold is the work of integrating the unconscious. Pearson's Creator at its mature end, corresponding to Anima stage four (Sophia — wisdom). Amber light in the hands, bottles and scrolls, a moon in the window. This is the Muse who builds.

## How the answers map onto the axes

The scoring table is hand-built. Each pickable option in the onboarding quiz is assigned to exactly one of the nine archetypes — a point of Hero-Warrior, a point of Muse-Lover, and so on. No claim is made that these assignments are psychometrically valid in the sense that a factor analysis would recover them. The claim is only that each assignment is *defensible on inspection*:

- **dinner date vs. coffee date.** The dinner date is Lover-coded: the long meal, the low light, the slowness of it. Coffee is Ruler-coded: short, public, scheduled.
- **moon vs. sun.** The moon is Witch-coded: the pale presider over the hidden. The sun is Ruler-coded: the visible center of the kingdom.
- **predator vs. prey.** Predator is Warrior-coded (direct, active, testing itself). Prey is Outcast-coded (wounded, watched, on the margins).
- **kitchen dancing vs. ballroom dancing.** Kitchen is Trickster-coded (informal, improvised). Ballroom is Ruler-coded (formal, held-together).
- **first touch vs. first kiss.** First touch is Warrior-coded (the new territory, the leap across a line). First kiss is Lover-coded (the sustained intimacy that follows once the line is past).
- **mirror (object).** Witch-coded; the mirror is the classical tool of scrying and self-knowledge.
- **fox (animal).** Trickster-coded, per basically every folk tradition north of the equator.

The full table lives in `constants/archetype-scoring.ts`. When in doubt about why a particular pair was placed the way it was: the side that implies *doing-in-the-world* is Hero; the side that implies *receiving-from-the-world* is Muse; the side that implies *hiding-from-the-world* is Shadow.

## Intentional reductions

**Why 9 and not 12.** Pearson has 12; we have 9. The drop isn't principled (all 12 are real); it's pragmatic. A tarot card needs a scene that reads at a glance. Caregiver, Sage, and Destroyer all generate scenes that are hard to distinguish from the ones we kept without resorting to costume-and-prop-level detail. 9 is also the largest number that makes a clean 3×3 table, which both the scoring and the composition exploit.

**Why normalize the scores.** Different archetypes have different numbers of reachable "slots" in the scoring table (range: 8–11 out of 84 total points). Without normalization, the archetypes with more slots would win more often just from the law of large numbers. We divide each archetype's raw score by its reachable-slot count so the winner is the one the user *over-indexed on*, not the one with the biggest catchment area.

**Why random-from-uid ties.** Sometimes the normalized top scores tie. The obvious solution — a fixed priority list — has a subtle failure mode: every near-tied user gets pushed toward the same archetype, which will show up in aggregate as an unexplained bump for whichever name sits first. We break ties instead by hashing the user's stable id, so every near-tie resolves on *the user*, not on a dev-defined preference.

**What this framework doesn't capture.** Big Five / OCEAN dimensions (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism) aren't in this at all. Neither are cultural or developmental variables. This is not a personality test. It's a nine-bucket symbolic reading, grounded in Jung's triad and Pearson's operationalization, that exists because a tarot-style card needs a single legible name. Read as personality research it is reductive; read as tarot it is thorough.
